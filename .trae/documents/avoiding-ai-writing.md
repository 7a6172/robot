# Comprehensive Guide to Recognizing and Avoiding Signs of AI-Generated Writing

## Purpose

This document provides a detailed guide to recognizing patterns commonly associated with AI-generated writing, especially text produced by large language models (LLMs).

It is based primarily on observed patterns documented in Wikipedia's guide to signs of AI writing, but it is written as a broader practical guide for:

- Writers
- Editors
- Researchers
- Content reviewers
- Documentation authors
- Developers
- Website owners
- AI-assisted writers
- Moderators
- Educators
- Anyone reviewing suspicious or low-quality generated text

The purpose is **not** to prove whether a piece of text was written by AI.

No single phrase, formatting choice, vocabulary word, or stylistic pattern can reliably prove AI authorship.

Instead, these indicators should be treated as signals that a text may require closer review.

The most important principle is:

> **Do not focus only on whether text looks AI-generated. Focus on whether the text is accurate, specific, properly sourced, appropriate for its purpose, and genuinely reviewed by a human.**

# Important Caveats

## AI detection is not certainty

One of the most important mistakes reviewers can make is assuming that suspicious writing automatically means AI generated the text.

This is not true.

Humans can:

- Write in generic language
- Overuse buzzwords
- Use repetitive sentence structures
- Write promotional copy
- Overuse headings
- Use excessive formatting
- Produce vague conclusions
- Repeat common phrases
- Make citation mistakes

AI models can also imitate human writing.

Therefore:

> **A sign of AI writing is not proof of AI writing.**

A good review process should investigate the quality and reliability of the text rather than attempting to make unsupported claims about authorship.

---

## AI detectors should not be treated as proof

Automated AI detectors may provide useful signals, but they can produce:

- False positives
- False negatives
- Unreliable confidence scores
- Different results after paraphrasing
- Different results after formatting changes
- Different results for different AI models

Minor changes can sometimes significantly affect detector results.

These include:

- Rephrasing
- Changing sentence order
- Adding markup
- Changing spacing
- Translating and retranslating text
- Editing vocabulary
- Combining human and AI writing

Therefore, a detector score should never be the only reason to conclude that content was generated by AI.

---

## Human intuition is also unreliable

People are often overconfident about their ability to recognize AI-generated text.

A passage that "feels like AI" may have been written by a human.

A passage that feels completely natural may have been heavily generated or assisted by AI.

For this reason:

- Do not rely only on instinct.
- Do not rely only on detectors.
- Do not rely only on vocabulary.
- Look for multiple indicators.
- Examine factual accuracy.
- Verify sources.
- Examine writing consistency.
- Check whether the author can explain editorial decisions.

---

## The indicators are descriptive, not prescriptive

This guide describes patterns that frequently appear in AI-generated writing.

It should not be interpreted as:

> "Avoid these words and your writing will no longer look AI-generated."

Simply replacing suspicious words can hide surface-level patterns without fixing deeper problems.

For example, replacing:

> "This highlights the significant role of the project."

with:

> "This demonstrates the important role of the project."

does not improve the underlying problem.

The actual question should be:

> What specific fact is being communicated?

If the sentence adds no meaningful information, it should usually be rewritten or removed.

# The Core Pattern Behind AI Writing

A major characteristic of AI-generated text is its tendency to produce language that is:

- Broadly applicable
- Statistically common
- Smooth
- Generic
- Positive
- Predictable
- Well-structured
- Insufficiently specific

This can create an unusual combination:

> **The writing sounds important while becoming less precise.**

For example:

### Specific and informative

> The company introduced its first battery-powered locomotive in 1964.

### Generic and inflated

> The company introduced a groundbreaking innovation that marked a pivotal moment in the evolution of transportation technology.

The second sentence sounds more impressive.

However, it provides less useful information.

This tendency can be described as a movement from:

**specific facts → generic significance**

Examples include:

- Specific achievement → "lasting legacy"
- Simple change → "pivotal transformation"
- Local event → "broader cultural shift"
- Ordinary organization → "dynamic hub of innovation"
- Minor feature → "testament to enduring importance"

When reviewing text, ask:

> Could this sentence apply to hundreds or thousands of unrelated subjects?

If the answer is yes, the sentence may be generic filler.

# Content-Level Indicators

Content-level indicators are often more important than individual vocabulary choices.

These include:

- Inflated importance
- Generic analysis
- Vague claims
- Unnecessary conclusions
- Promotional language
- Artificial discussion of recognition
- Overgeneralized opinions
- Unsupported interpretation
- Generic future prospects

---

# Exaggerated Significance and Importance

AI-generated text frequently emphasizes a subject's importance even when the available facts do not justify such language.

## Common patterns

Watch for phrases such as:

- stands as
- serves as
- is a testament to
- serves as a reminder of
- plays a crucial role
- plays a pivotal role
- plays a vital role
- marks a significant moment
- marks a key turning point
- represents a major shift
- underscores its importance
- highlights its significance
- reflects broader trends
- symbolizes
- contributes to
- shapes the landscape
- sets the stage for
- leaves an indelible mark
- has a lasting impact
- has an enduring legacy
- remains deeply rooted
- continues to influence

None of these phrases is inherently wrong.

The problem arises when they are:

- Unsupported
- Repetitive
- Generic
- Unnecessary
- Applied to trivial facts
- Used instead of explaining concrete consequences

---

## Example

### Generic

> The organization's founding marked a pivotal moment in the evolution of regional development.

Questions to ask:

- Pivotal according to whom?
- What changed?
- What evidence shows it was pivotal?
- Which regional development?
- How did the founding affect it?

### Better

> The organization was established in 1989 and became responsible for publishing regional economic statistics previously compiled by national agencies.

The improved version:

- States the fact
- Explains the consequence
- Avoids unsupported importance claims

---

## Warning signs

Be cautious when ordinary facts are followed by dramatic interpretation.

For example:

> The road was completed in 1972, marking an important milestone in the region's transportation development.

If the source merely says the road was completed in 1972, then "marking an important milestone" may be unsupported interpretation.

---

## Common AI pattern: importance inflation

AI may take a simple fact:

> The museum opened in 1995.

And transform it into:

> The museum opened in 1995, marking a significant milestone in the preservation and celebration of the region's rich cultural heritage.

The additional language may sound sophisticated, but it does not necessarily add information.

---

# Artificial Claims of Notability and Recognition

AI-generated content may attempt to prove that a subject is important by describing the sources that mention it.

Common phrases include:

- independent coverage
- widely covered
- featured in
- profiled by
- recognized by
- national media
- regional media
- prominent outlets
- respected publications
- leading experts
- trade publications
- significant media attention
- active social media presence

---

## Why this can be suspicious

Instead of simply describing the subject, the text begins explaining why the subject deserves attention.

For example:

> The organization has received extensive coverage from respected national and regional media outlets.

This raises several questions:

- Which outlets?
- What did they cover?
- Was the coverage substantial?
- Is the statement supported?
- Why is the classification of the outlets relevant?

The text may be attempting to argue for notability rather than neutrally describe the subject.

---

## Common pattern: source-focused writing

AI may write paragraphs that focus more on:

- The existence of sources
- The type of sources
- The reliability of sources
- The prominence of sources

than on what those sources actually say.

For example:

### Weak

> The subject has been featured in multiple independent and widely respected publications, demonstrating substantial public recognition.

### Better

> A 2024 profile in Publication X described the subject's work on Y.

The second version communicates actual information.

---

# Superficial Analysis and Empty Interpretation

One of the strongest content-level indicators is the insertion of analysis that sounds meaningful but provides little actual information.

These statements often follow factual information.

## Common pattern

> Fact, **highlighting the importance of...**

> Fact, **underscoring its significance...**

> Fact, **reflecting broader trends...**

> Fact, **demonstrating its continued relevance...**

> Fact, **contributing to...**

> Fact, **fostering...**

> Fact, **enhancing...**

---

## Example

> The station has eight platforms, highlighting its importance as a major transportation hub.

The factual part is:

> The station has eight platforms.

The interpretation is:

> highlighting its importance as a major transportation hub.

Questions:

- Does eight platforms automatically prove importance?
- Is "major transportation hub" supported by a source?
- Does the sentence explain anything new?

Often, the interpretive clause should be removed unless a reliable source explicitly makes the connection.

---

## Common "-ing" construction

AI-generated writing frequently attaches a present-participle phrase to the end of a sentence.

Examples:

- highlighting
- underscoring
- reflecting
- symbolizing
- contributing
- ensuring
- fostering
- enhancing
- demonstrating
- showcasing
- emphasizing

Example:

> The program expanded in 2022, reflecting the organization's commitment to innovation.

This can be suspicious when the second clause is:

- Vague
- Promotional
- Unsupported
- Automatically inferred from the first clause

---

## Better approach

Replace interpretation with evidence.

### Weak

> The expansion reflected the company's commitment to innovation.

### Better

> The expansion added two research laboratories and increased the company's research staff by 30 percent.

Let the evidence communicate the significance.

---

# Promotional and Advertisement-Like Language

AI-generated text frequently adopts the tone of:

- Marketing copy
- Tourism promotion
- Corporate brochures
- Public relations material
- Product descriptions

## Words and phrases to watch

Examples include:

- boasts
- vibrant
- rich
- profound
- remarkable
- renowned
- groundbreaking
- world-class
- innovative
- diverse array
- stunning
- breathtaking
- picturesque
- nestled
- in the heart of
- natural beauty
- commitment to excellence
- showcases
- offers visitors
- unique blend
- thriving
- dynamic
- premier
- exceptional
- transformative

Again, these words are not automatically evidence of AI writing.

The problem is their **density and purpose**.

---

## Example

### Promotional

> Nestled in the heart of the city, the vibrant destination offers visitors a unique blend of rich culture, stunning architecture, and unforgettable experiences.

This could describe hundreds of cities, hotels, museums, or tourist attractions.

### Informative

> The district contains several nineteenth-century buildings and receives approximately 500,000 visitors annually.

The second version provides verifiable information.

---

## Common AI pattern: positive default tone

AI models often assume that subjects should be described positively.

This can lead to:

- Excessive praise
- Uncritical descriptions
- Inflated achievements
- Promotional adjectives
- Generic admiration

Ask:

> Would this sentence sound appropriate in an encyclopedia, academic article, technical document, or neutral report?

If it sounds like advertising, rewrite it.

---

# Vague Attribution and Overgeneralization

AI-generated text may make statements about what people supposedly believe without identifying who those people are.

## Examples

- Experts believe...
- Critics argue...
- Many consider...
- It is widely regarded as...
- The project is seen as...
- Observers have noted...
- Researchers suggest...
- Some have described...
- The public views...
- The organization is recognized as...

These phrases are not automatically wrong.

However, they should raise questions.

---

## Ask

- Which experts?
- Which critics?
- How many people?
- According to which source?
- Is there actually a documented consensus?
- Is the statement an interpretation created by the writer?

---

## Weak

> The building is widely regarded as an important cultural landmark.

### Better

> The city's heritage register designated the building as a protected cultural landmark in 2018.

The second statement provides:

- A specific actor
- A specific action
- A specific date
- A verifiable claim

---

## Overgeneralization

AI may transform limited evidence into broad conclusions.

For example:

### Evidence

One newspaper reviewed an event.

### AI-style conclusion

> The event received widespread recognition and significant media attention.

The conclusion may not follow from the evidence.

Always distinguish between:

- What happened
- What a source explicitly states
- What the writer is inferring

---

# Generic Conclusions About Challenges and the Future

AI-generated articles often end with predictable paragraphs about:

- Challenges
- Opportunities
- Future development
- Continued evolution
- Innovation
- Sustainability
- Growth prospects

These conclusions can appear even when the subject does not require them.

---

## Common structure

> Despite ongoing challenges, the organization remains well positioned for future growth and continued innovation.

This sentence is generic.

Ask:

- Which challenges?
- What evidence supports them?
- What future plans exist?
- Who says growth is expected?
- What does "well positioned" mean?

---

## Common phrases

Watch for:

- Looking ahead
- Moving forward
- Future prospects
- Continued growth
- Ongoing challenges
- Emerging opportunities
- Evolving landscape
- As technology continues to evolve
- The future remains promising
- Continued innovation
- Future development
- Adaptation will be essential

---

## Better approach

Only include future discussion when:

- The future plans are documented
- Reliable sources discuss them
- They are relevant to the subject
- Specific facts can be provided

### Weak

> Looking ahead, the industry faces both challenges and opportunities.

### Better

> The industry's 2025 strategic plan identified labor shortages and energy costs as major risks through 2030.

---

# Artificial Awards and Recognition Sections

AI-generated articles may automatically create sections titled:

- Awards
- Recognition
- Awards and recognition
- Honors
- Achievements
- Accolades

The existence of such a section is not itself suspicious.

The problem occurs when it contains:

- Minor mentions
- Promotional claims
- Lists of weak distinctions
- Repeated media appearances
- Unverified awards
- Awards created mainly to make the subject appear notable

---

## Review questions

For every award:

- Does it actually exist?
- Is the recipient correctly identified?
- Is the award relevant?
- Is the source reliable?
- Is the award independent?
- Is the information duplicated elsewhere?

Do not create a recognition section simply because the subject has received praise or media attention.

# Language and Vocabulary Indicators

AI writing can develop recognizable vocabulary patterns.

The strongest signal is usually **density**, not the presence of one word.

For example, one use of "pivotal" is normal.

Five uses in three paragraphs may indicate formulaic writing.

---

# High Density of AI-Associated Vocabulary

Certain words and phrases frequently appear in generated text.

Examples include:

- pivotal
- crucial
- vital
- significant
- key
- underscore
- highlight
- showcase
- foster
- facilitate
- enhance
- resonate
- align
- navigate
- evolving
- landscape
- multifaceted
- nuanced
- dynamic
- comprehensive
- transformative
- enduring
- robust
- innovative
- vibrant
- seamless
- impactful

These words are legitimate English vocabulary.

The issue is repetitive clustering.

---

## Example of excessive density

> The pivotal initiative played a crucial role in shaping the dynamic and evolving landscape, highlighting the organization's enduring commitment to innovation.

This sentence contains many impressive-sounding words but little concrete information.

---

## Better

> The initiative introduced a new funding program for local research projects.

---

# Avoidance of Simple Direct Language

AI-generated writing may avoid simple verbs such as:

- is
- are
- was
- were

Instead, it may prefer more elaborate constructions.

For example:

### Simple

> The company is based in Singapore.

### Inflated

> The company stands as a Singapore-based organization operating within the region's evolving business landscape.

The first sentence is clearer.

---

## Why this happens

Generated writing often attempts to sound:

- More formal
- More sophisticated
- More varied
- Less repetitive

This can result in unnatural complexity.

Do not avoid simple language merely to sound more advanced.

Simple statements are often better.

---

# Vague Connections and Associations

AI frequently creates weak relationships between facts.

Examples include:

- associated with
- connected to
- linked to
- tied to
- reflects
- resonates with
- aligns with
- contributes to
- forms part of
- represents

These connections may sound meaningful without explaining an actual relationship.

---

## Example

> The architecture reflects the city's cultural identity.

Questions:

- How?
- According to whom?
- Which architectural features?
- Which aspects of cultural identity?

---

## Better

> The building incorporates motifs used in traditional local textile patterns.

Specific relationships are stronger than abstract associations.

---

# Negative Parallelisms and Formulaic Contrasts

AI writing frequently uses rhetorical contrast structures.

These can become repetitive and artificial.

---

## "Not just X, but also Y"

Example:

> The project is not just a technological achievement, but also a symbol of international cooperation.

This structure often adds unnecessary drama.

Use it sparingly.

---

## "Not X, but Y"

Example:

> The event was not merely a celebration, but a defining moment for the community.

Ask whether the second phrase is supported or simply exaggerated.

---

## "X rather than Y"

Example:

> The organization focuses on collaboration rather than competition.

This may be perfectly valid, but repeated rhetorical contrasts can make writing sound generated.

---

## Why this pattern is common

AI often uses contrast to create:

- Flow
- Emphasis
- Balance
- Rhetorical sophistication

However, excessive contrast can produce a predictable style.

---

# The Rule of Three

Generated writing often groups concepts into threes.

Examples:

- innovation, sustainability, and growth
- culture, history, and identity
- challenges, opportunities, and solutions
- efficiency, reliability, and accessibility

Humans also use groups of three naturally.

The problem is excessive repetition.

---

## Example

> The program promotes innovation, collaboration, and sustainability.

Ask whether all three concepts are necessary.

Sometimes a more specific statement is better.

> The program funds joint research projects between universities and local companies.

---

# Formatting and Structural Indicators

AI-generated content often follows predictable structures.

This may include:

- Too many headings
- Perfectly balanced sections
- Excessive lists
- Repeated formatting patterns
- Artificial summaries
- Overuse of bold text
- Decorative emojis
- Unnecessary tables

---

# Overuse of Headings

AI frequently divides content into many sections.

Example:

```text
# Introduction

# History

## Early History

## Later History

# Impact

## Cultural Impact

## Social Impact

# Challenges

# Future Prospects

# Conclusion
```

This may be appropriate for a large report.

However, for a short article, it can create unnecessary fragmentation.

---

## Common problem: headings containing only other headings

Example:

```text
## History

### Early history

### Modern history
```

The parent heading contains no actual content.

This may indicate outline-first generation.

---

## Better

Either:

- Add meaningful introductory content under the parent heading, or
- Remove the unnecessary parent heading.

---

# Title Case and Heading Problems

AI-generated documents may apply title case inconsistently or excessively.

For example:

```text
## The Importance Of Artificial Intelligence
```

instead of:

```text
## The importance of artificial intelligence
```

depending on the relevant style guide.

The issue is not AI detection alone.

It may indicate that content was produced from generic templates rather than edited for the target platform.

---

# Overuse of Bold Text

AI assistants often emphasize key phrases using bold formatting.

Example:

> The system provides **high performance**, **scalability**, and **reliability**.

Repeated bold formatting can make documents resemble generated summaries or marketing material.

---

## Common patterns

- Every important noun is bold
- Multiple bold phrases per paragraph
- Entire sentences are bold
- Bold text replaces proper structure
- Bold labels are used as artificial headings

Use bold text only when it serves a clear purpose.

---

# Artificial Lists and Inline Headers

AI frequently creates lists like:

> **Performance:** The system processes data quickly.  
> **Scalability:** The system can handle larger workloads.  
> **Reliability:** The system reduces failures.

This can be useful.

However, excessive use can make a document feel like a generated response template.

Ask whether:

- A table would be better
- Normal paragraphs would be better
- Real headings would be better
- The list is necessary

---

# Overuse of Em Dashes

AI-generated writing frequently uses em dashes to:

- Add explanations
- Insert dramatic pauses
- Introduce qualifications
- Connect related ideas

Example:

> The system—which was developed over several years—represents a major advancement.

Again, em dashes are legitimate punctuation.

The issue is repeated use.

If nearly every paragraph contains em dashes, consider whether simpler punctuation would improve readability.

---

# Emoji as Structural Formatting

AI-generated answers often use emojis as section markers.

Example:

```text
🚀 Features

📊 Performance

⚠️ Limitations

🔮 Future Plans
```

This may be appropriate for:

- Social media
- Informal documentation
- Presentations
- Tutorials

It may be inappropriate for:

- Encyclopedic content
- Academic writing
- Formal reports
- Neutral documentation

The key issue is whether the formatting matches the intended audience and platform.

---

# Unusual Tables

AI frequently converts information into tables even when a table is unnecessary.

Example:

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| Speed | Fast |
| Reliability | Reliable |
| Innovation | Innovative |

This table adds little value.

Tables should be used when they help compare structured information.

Avoid tables that merely restate ordinary prose.

---

# Heading-Level Problems

AI-generated Markdown may skip heading levels.

Example:

```markdown
# Main Section

### Subsection
```

without an intermediate level.

This can indicate generated formatting that was not carefully reviewed.

Use logical heading hierarchies:

```markdown
# Main Section

## Subsection

### Sub-subsection
```

---

# Overuse of Top-Level Headings

Generated documents sometimes use many level-one headings:

```markdown
# Introduction

# Features

# Installation

# Usage

# Conclusion
```

Depending on the platform, only one top-level title may be appropriate.

The rest should use lower-level headings.

---

# Excessive Section Breaks

AI may insert horizontal rules repeatedly:

```markdown
---

## Section One

---

## Section Two

---

## Section Three
```

This can make a document feel artificially segmented.

Use thematic breaks only when they serve a real structural purpose.

---

# Communication Intended for the User Instead of the Reader

One of the clearest signs of accidentally copied AI output is text that still communicates with the original user.

Examples:

- Here is a revised version:
- You can use the following:
- Let me know if you would like...
- I hope this helps.
- Based on your request...
- Here are several options:
- You may want to consider...
- As requested...

These statements belong to a conversation, not usually to the final published content.

---

## Example

### AI-response artifact

> Here is a neutral version suitable for publication:

> The company was founded in 1985.

If copied into a document, the first sentence is an artifact.

Remove conversational framing unless the document itself requires it.

---

# Collaborative Communication

AI assistants often use collaborative language.

Examples:

- We can...
- Let's explore...
- We should...
- Together, we can...
- Here's how we can approach this...

This may be appropriate in:

- Tutorials
- Interactive guides
- Team documentation

It may be inappropriate in neutral published content.

---

# Knowledge-Cutoff Disclaimers

AI-generated text may contain statements such as:

- As of my knowledge cutoff...
- I do not have access to...
- My information may not be current...
- Based on information available to me...

These statements are usually artifacts of an AI response.

They should not normally appear in final content unless the document specifically discusses AI limitations.

---

# Placeholder Text and Response Templates

Generated content may include placeholders such as:

- [Insert Name]
- [Date]
- [Source needed]
- XYZ
- Company Name
- Your Organization
- Example Name
- [Add details here]

Placeholders are not necessarily evidence of AI.

However, unexplained placeholders may indicate that generated output was copied without review.

Before publishing:

- Search for brackets
- Search for TODO
- Search for FIXME
- Search for "insert"
- Search for "placeholder"
- Search for generic names

---

# Markup and Formatting Artifacts

One of the strongest technical indicators of AI-assisted content is the presence of broken internal formatting.

These may include:

- Markdown copied into incompatible systems
- Broken HTML
- Broken wiki markup
- Citation placeholders
- Internal model references
- Tool output artifacts

---

# Use of Markdown in the Wrong Context

AI assistants frequently produce Markdown by default.

Examples include:

```markdown
## Heading

**Bold text**

- Bullet point
```

If this is pasted into a platform that does not support Markdown, the raw symbols may remain visible.

Review all generated content for:

- `**`
- `##`
- `###`
- Backticks
- Markdown links
- Markdown tables
- Blockquotes

---

# Broken Wikitext or Platform-Specific Markup

Generated content may incorrectly imitate markup syntax.

Possible problems include:

- Missing brackets
- Incorrect template syntax
- Invalid references
- Broken internal links
- Nonexistent templates
- Incorrect categories

Never assume AI-generated markup is valid.

Validate it in the actual target environment.

---

# Internal Formatting and Reference Markup Bugs

Some AI systems may accidentally expose internal technical markers.

Examples can include:

- Tool-reference identifiers
- Citation placeholders
- Internal search IDs
- Content-reference markers
- Rendering instructions
- File-upload identifiers
- Structured metadata

These artifacts are especially strong evidence that AI output was copied directly without human review.

---

## Review for suspicious strings

Search for unusual fragments such as:

- citation placeholders
- internal search references
- model-specific tags
- rendering instructions
- content references
- file upload identifiers
- tool-generated metadata
- unexplained brackets or symbols

If these appear in published content, investigate their origin.

---

# Nonexistent or Out-of-Place Categories

In systems that use categories or tags, AI may invent categories that:

- Do not exist
- Are incorrectly named
- Are inappropriate
- Are unrelated
- Follow an imagined naming pattern

Always verify categories against the actual platform.

---

# Nonexistent Templates

AI may invent templates because it predicts what a template name should look like.

For example, it may generate something that appears structurally plausible but does not exist.

Never assume that:

> "This looks like a valid template name"

means:

> "This template exists."

Verify every platform-specific construct.

---

# Broken References and Citation Problems

Citation problems are among the most serious risks of AI-generated content.

Unlike style problems, citation failures can directly affect factual reliability.

---

# Broken External Links

AI may generate:

- Nonexistent URLs
- Incorrect URLs
- URLs with invented paths
- Broken domains
- Search-result URLs instead of source URLs
- URLs containing tracking artifacts

Always open and verify links.

Do not assume that a realistic-looking URL exists.

---

# Fabricated or Invalid Identifiers

AI may hallucinate:

- DOI numbers
- ISBNs
- Article numbers
- Volume numbers
- Page numbers
- Publication dates
- Archive identifiers

These can look convincing.

Verify identifiers through reliable databases or the original publication.

---

# DOI Problems

A DOI may be:

- Invalid
- Mistyped
- Real but unrelated
- Associated with a different article

Checking whether a DOI resolves is not enough.

Also verify that the resolved publication actually supports the claim.

---

# ISBN Problems

An ISBN may:

- Be invalid
- Refer to another edition
- Refer to another book
- Contain a digit error

Validate the identifier and bibliographic information.

---

# Book Citations Without Useful Location Information

AI-generated citations may cite an entire book without:

- Page numbers
- Chapter numbers
- Relevant sections
- URLs
- Edition information

This makes verification difficult.

Where appropriate, include enough information for readers to locate the relevant claim.

---

# Incorrect Citation Usage

AI may place citations near claims that the sources do not actually support.

This is particularly dangerous because the presence of a citation can make unsupported text appear credible.

Common problems include:

- Citation does not mention the claim
- Citation supports only part of the sentence
- Citation is attached to AI-generated interpretation
- Citation is from an unrelated source
- Citation is copied from a search result snippet
- Citation supports a weaker claim than the text makes

---

## Review process

For every important claim:

1. Open the source.
2. Find the relevant information.
3. Compare the source with the wording.
4. Remove unsupported interpretation.
5. Verify dates, names, and numbers.
6. Ensure the citation is attached to the correct statement.

---

# Tracking Parameters and Search Links

Generated links may contain unnecessary parameters such as tracking or source identifiers.

Examples include:

- search parameters
- AI-source tracking parameters
- referral identifiers
- temporary session parameters

Prefer stable, canonical source URLs where possible.

Do not cite a search-results page when the original source is available.

---

# Named References That Are Never Used

Generated markup may define references or resources that never appear in the actual content.

This can happen when AI:

- Generates a bibliography first
- Produces more citations than necessary
- Removes claims without removing references
- Copies a template incorrectly

Review for unused references.

---

# Comment and Discussion Indicators

AI-generated comments can have different indicators from AI-generated articles.

Common patterns include:

- Excessive politeness
- Generic collaboration language
- Long procedural explanations
- Repetition of policy names
- Overexplaining obvious actions
- Perfectly structured responses to simple questions
- Artificial neutrality

---

## Example

A simple human response might be:

> I disagree because the source does not support that sentence.

An AI-style response might be:

> Thank you for raising this important concern. I appreciate the opportunity to clarify the issue and ensure that the content remains fully aligned with applicable guidelines and sourcing standards.

The second response is not necessarily AI-generated.

However, if this tone appears repeatedly, it may indicate generated or heavily templated communication.

---

# AI-Like Edit Summaries and Procedural Language

Generated summaries may overexplain the editing process.

Examples include:

- Ensured compliance with guidelines
- Preserved all relevant information
- Retained important details
- Avoided unsupported claims
- Improved neutrality
- Maintained citation integrity
- Followed formatting requirements

A human edit summary often focuses simply on what changed.

Example:

> Removed unsupported claim and added source.

The AI-style version may instead explain intentions and process in excessive detail.

---

## Canned assurances

Be cautious of repeated statements claiming:

- Compliance
- Accuracy
- Neutrality
- Proper sourcing
- Guideline adherence

Especially when the actual content has not been independently checked.

A statement that something follows a rule does not prove that it does.

---

# Sudden Changes in Writing Style

A sudden change in writing style can indicate:

- AI assistance
- Different authors
- Copy-paste from another source
- Major editing
- Translation
- Template use

Examples of changes include:

- Vocabulary suddenly becoming more elaborate
- Sentence length changing dramatically
- Tone becoming unusually formal
- Excessive headings appearing
- New sections using promotional language
- Citation style changing
- Grammar suddenly improving or worsening

---

## Important caveat

A style shift alone is not proof of AI use.

People naturally write differently depending on:

- Time
- Topic
- Language
- Editing
- Audience
- Experience
- Collaboration

Investigate the quality of the content rather than making assumptions about authorship.

---

# Signs of Human Writing

Some features can provide evidence that text was genuinely written or substantially reviewed by a human.

These are also not absolute proof.

---

## Text predating modern public AI systems

Older text may predate certain AI tools.

This does not prove human authorship because:

- Other automated systems existed
- The text may have been modified later
- Dates may be misleading

However, the age of a text can provide useful context.

---

## Ability to explain editorial choices

A human editor may be able to explain:

- Why a sentence was added
- Why a source was selected
- Why information was removed
- Why a particular structure was chosen
- How a conclusion was reached

This can be more informative than trying to judge writing style.

---

## Human irregularity

Human writing often contains:

- Personal habits
- Uneven sentence structures
- Specific priorities
- Unexpected word choices
- Topic-specific knowledge
- Natural imperfections
- Genuine editorial judgment

However, polished human writing can also appear highly structured.

Do not assume:

> imperfect = human

or:

> polished = AI

---

# Ineffective Indicators

Some commonly suggested AI indicators are weak or unreliable.

Examples may include:

- A single unusual word
- Good grammar
- Formal tone
- Long sentences
- Short sentences
- Use of semicolons
- Use of em dashes
- Use of headings
- Use of certain adjectives
- Lack of spelling mistakes
- "It sounds like ChatGPT"

These indicators become more useful only when combined with other evidence.

---

# Historical AI-Writing Indicators

AI writing styles change over time.

Some indicators that were common in earlier models may become less useful as systems improve.

---

# Didactic Disclaimers

Earlier AI responses often included educational disclaimers such as:

- It is important to note that...
- It should be noted that...
- Please keep in mind that...
- It is worth mentioning that...

These phrases are still used by humans, but repeated use can make writing feel generated.

---

# Section Summaries

Older generated content often ended every section with a summary.

Example:

> In summary, this section demonstrates the importance of...

Excessive summaries can create repetitive structure.

Use summaries when they help the reader.

Do not automatically summarize every section.

---

# Prompt Refusal Artifacts

Older AI-generated output sometimes accidentally included statements such as:

- I cannot...
- I am unable to...
- As an AI...
- I don't have access to...

These clearly belong to the generation process rather than the final content.

Remove them unless they are directly relevant to the document.

---

# Abrupt Cutoffs

Generated text may sometimes end abruptly because:

- Output was truncated
- A response limit was reached
- Copying stopped early
- Generation failed

Check documents for:

- Incomplete sentences
- Missing sections
- Broken lists
- Unfinished citations
- Sudden endings

---

# Outdated Access-Date Patterns

Automated systems may generate dates using:

- Incorrect current dates
- Default dates
- Future dates
- Inconsistent date formats

Always verify:

- Publication date
- Access date
- Archive date
- Retrieval date

---

# Lexical Diversity and "Elegant Variation"

AI systems may avoid repeating the same word by constantly substituting synonyms.

This can create unnatural variation.

For example, a document may refer to the same thing as:

- organization
- institution
- entity
- body
- establishment
- initiative

even when simple repetition would be clearer.

---

## Better approach

Do not avoid repetition when repetition improves clarity.

Technical writing often benefits from consistent terminology.

If the subject is a:

> database

do not repeatedly call it:

- data repository
- information platform
- storage ecosystem
- digital infrastructure

unless those terms genuinely mean different things.

---

# A Practical Review Workflow

When reviewing potentially AI-generated content, use a structured process.

---

## Step 1: Ignore authorship initially

Ask:

> Is this good content?

Check:

- Accuracy
- Relevance
- Clarity
- Specificity
- Neutrality
- Sources

Do not begin with:

> Was this written by AI?

---

## Step 2: Look for generic content

Highlight sentences that could apply to many unrelated subjects.

Example:

> The organization continues to play an important role in the evolving landscape of the industry.

Ask:

- What organization?
- What role?
- Which industry?
- What evidence?
- What changed?

---

## Step 3: Identify inflated language

Search for:

- pivotal
- significant
- crucial
- vital
- groundbreaking
- enduring
- transformative
- vibrant
- dynamic

Then determine whether each use is justified.

---

## Step 4: Check interpretive phrases

Look for:

- highlighting
- underscoring
- reflecting
- symbolizing
- demonstrating
- contributing
- fostering

Ask whether the interpretation is explicitly supported.

---

## Step 5: Verify every important source

Do not trust citations merely because they exist.

Open the sources.

Check:

- Existence
- Relevance
- Accuracy
- Context
- Claim support

---

## Step 6: Examine structure

Look for:

- Too many headings
- Empty parent sections
- Repetitive lists
- Excessive summaries
- Unnecessary tables
- Decorative formatting

---

## Step 7: Search for technical artifacts

Search the raw text for:

- Markdown
- Internal citations
- Placeholder text
- Tool identifiers
- Broken references
- Template instructions
- Unusual metadata

---

## Step 8: Compare writing style

Look for sudden changes in:

- Tone
- Vocabulary
- Formatting
- Grammar
- Complexity

Do not treat this as proof.

Use it as a reason for closer review.

---

## Step 9: Investigate deeper problems

The most important question is:

> What caused the suspicious text?

Possible underlying problems include:

- Fabricated facts
- Fabricated citations
- Unsupported interpretation
- Copying without review
- Promotional content
- Original research
- Source misrepresentation
- Poor editorial judgment

Fix the underlying issue.

Do not merely replace "AI words."

---

# How to Improve AI-Assisted Writing

AI can be useful as a writing assistant, but output should not be published without meaningful human review.

---

## Principle 1: Start with facts

Provide:

- Reliable sources
- Verified notes
- Accurate data
- Clear constraints

Do not ask an AI to invent missing information.

---

## Principle 2: Preserve specificity

Prefer:

> The software added support for CUDA 12.4.

over:

> The software introduced significant enhancements that strengthened its technological capabilities.

Specific information is more useful.

---

## Principle 3: Remove unsupported importance claims

Delete phrases such as:

- marking a pivotal moment
- highlighting its significance
- demonstrating its importance

unless supported by evidence.

---

## Principle 4: Use simple language

Prefer:

> The project uses PostgreSQL.

over:

> The project leverages PostgreSQL as a robust database solution.

Prefer:

> The company is based in Singapore.

over:

> The company operates from its strategic base within Singapore's dynamic business landscape.

---

## Principle 5: Do not manufacture analysis

If a source says:

> The building opened in 1980.

Do not automatically write:

> The building opened in 1980, symbolizing a new era of architectural development.

Only include analysis when:

- It is explicitly supported
- It is properly attributed
- It provides real value

---

## Principle 6: Avoid automatic conclusions

Do not automatically end articles with:

- challenges
- opportunities
- future prospects
- continued evolution

Only include these topics when they are genuinely relevant.

---

## Principle 7: Verify citations independently

Never assume AI-generated citations are correct.

For every source:

1. Confirm it exists.
2. Confirm the author.
3. Confirm the date.
4. Confirm the title.
5. Confirm the link.
6. Confirm the source supports the claim.

---

## Principle 8: Edit for the target platform

Remove formatting that belongs to another environment.

Examples:

- Markdown in plain text
- Chat-style responses in articles
- Internal AI citations
- Decorative emojis in formal documentation
- Generic response headings

---

## Principle 9: Keep terminology consistent

Do not replace words with synonyms merely to avoid repetition.

Consistency is usually more important than artificial vocabulary variety.

---

## Principle 10: Add genuine human judgment

Human review should involve more than:

- Correcting grammar
- Changing a few words
- Removing obvious AI phrases

A reviewer should actively decide:

- What information belongs
- What information should be removed
- Which sources are trustworthy
- How claims should be framed
- What the reader actually needs

---

# A Detailed AI-Writing Review Checklist

## Content

- [ ] Does every paragraph contain specific information?
- [ ] Could generic sentences apply to many unrelated subjects?
- [ ] Are importance claims supported?
- [ ] Are broad conclusions necessary?
- [ ] Is the subject described neutrally?
- [ ] Are promotional claims removed?
- [ ] Is interpretation supported by sources?
- [ ] Are vague opinions attributed?

---

## Language

- [ ] Is vocabulary unnecessarily elaborate?
- [ ] Are words like "pivotal" and "significant" overused?
- [ ] Are phrases such as "highlighting" or "underscoring" repeated?
- [ ] Is simple language avoided unnecessarily?
- [ ] Are rhetorical contrasts overused?
- [ ] Is the rule-of-three pattern repetitive?
- [ ] Is terminology consistent?

---

## Structure

- [ ] Are there too many headings?
- [ ] Does every parent heading contain meaningful content?
- [ ] Are heading levels logical?
- [ ] Are unnecessary summaries removed?
- [ ] Are lists genuinely useful?
- [ ] Are tables necessary?
- [ ] Are horizontal rules overused?

---

## Formatting

- [ ] Is bold text used sparingly?
- [ ] Are em dashes overused?
- [ ] Are emojis appropriate?
- [ ] Is the formatting appropriate for the platform?
- [ ] Has raw Markdown been removed where unsupported?

---

## Sources

- [ ] Does every important citation exist?
- [ ] Does every citation support its claim?
- [ ] Are URLs valid?
- [ ] Are DOIs correct?
- [ ] Are ISBNs correct?
- [ ] Are page numbers included when needed?
- [ ] Are citations attached to the correct statements?
- [ ] Have search-result links been replaced with original sources?

---

## AI Artifacts

- [ ] Are internal citations removed?
- [ ] Are tool identifiers removed?
- [ ] Are placeholders removed?
- [ ] Are chat-response phrases removed?
- [ ] Are knowledge-cutoff disclaimers removed?
- [ ] Are response templates removed?
- [ ] Are broken markup artifacts removed?

---

## Human Review

- [ ] Can the writer explain why each major claim exists?
- [ ] Can the writer explain the source selection?
- [ ] Has someone checked the factual claims?
- [ ] Has someone reviewed the text for hallucinated information?
- [ ] Has someone reviewed the text for unsupported interpretation?
- [ ] Has someone checked the final rendering?

# Quick Detection Heuristics

The following combination should trigger closer review:

1. **Generic but impressive language**
2. **Frequent claims of importance**
3. **Heavy use of words like "pivotal" and "significant"**
4. **Sentences ending in "highlighting," "underscoring," or "reflecting" clauses**
5. **Promotional tone**
6. **Vague claims about what experts or people believe**
7. **Artificial sections about challenges and future prospects**
8. **Too many headings**
9. **Excessive bold text**
10. **Broken citations or markup**
11. **Internal AI artifacts**
12. **Sources that do not support the claims**
13. **Sudden changes in writing style**

The presence of one or two indicators means very little.

The presence of multiple indicators combined with factual or citation problems is much more significant.

---

# Recommended Editing Philosophy

Do not try to make writing "look less AI."

Instead, make it:

- More accurate
- More specific
- Better sourced
- Less repetitive
- Less promotional
- More appropriate for its audience
- Easier to verify
- More concise
- More genuinely edited

A useful rule is:

> **Replace vague significance with specific facts.**

Instead of:

> The project represents a significant advancement in the field.

Write:

> The project reduced processing time from 12 hours to 45 minutes.

Instead of:

> The organization plays a vital role in the community.

Write:

> The organization operates three community clinics serving approximately 8,000 patients annually.

Instead of:

> The initiative highlights the importance of sustainability.

Write:

> The initiative reduced annual water consumption by 18 percent.

---

# Final Principle

The best way to review suspicious AI-like writing is not to hunt for forbidden words or stylistic tricks.

The strongest review process asks:

- Is this true?
- Is this specific?
- Is this supported?
- Is this necessary?
- Does the source actually say this?
- Does the writing fit the intended audience?
- Has a human made meaningful editorial decisions?

A sentence can sound completely natural and still be false.

A sentence can sound AI-generated and still be accurate.

Therefore, the goal should always be:

> **Improve the quality and reliability of the content rather than merely hiding or removing signs associated with AI writing.**

---

# One-Sentence Summary

**AI-like writing is most recognizable not because of any single word or formatting habit, but because generic language, inflated significance, superficial interpretation, repetitive structure, technical artifacts, and weak source verification can combine into a pattern that requires closer human review.**
